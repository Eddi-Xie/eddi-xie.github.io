Two-arm imitation-learning rig

Fall 2026, in progress.

Two SO-101 arms from the LeRobot ecosystem, five degrees of freedom each, printed on my own printer, assembled and calibrated, with compliant TPU fingers (not yet installed in the photos). Leader–follower teleoperation has worked since Sept 20, 2026: I move the leader by hand and the follower copies it.

Goals for the project come in three tiers. Tier 1, by Dec 13, 2026: on my kitchen island, the arm recognizes about six kitchen items and puts each where it belongs, running an imitation-learning policy trained on my own teleoperated demonstrations. Tier 2 adds object pose tracking, camera-based hand tracking and a load-cell admittance loop for home chores. Tier 3 puts it on a mobile base, with hardware upgrades, for kitchen prep tasks.

I have also incorporated the arm into my perception course project for active vision in imitation learning: can a robot decide where to look to help itself in its task? Instead of the typical fixed camera or one that is learned from human demonstrations, I designed the camera arm to steer itself in the direction where an ensemble of five ACT policies disagrees less (uncertainty-driven). The same policy is used to drive the task arms and is isolated from the camera controller. Therefore, I can change the camera controls and attribute the changes in success to the camera. I have segmented the project into three stages: (1) in simulation (AV-ALOHA, in MuJoCo), (2) on the real two-arm rig shown above, and (3) with both arms acting as the camera arm and the task arm simultaneously and helping each other reach views to benefit one another.

As of now, I have acquired the first results from the simulation stage by utilizing a Claude Code workflow as an implementation agent. The results showed that the uncertainty-driven camera succeeded in 42.0% of the 1,000 evaluated scenes compared to 29.6% for a fixed-camera baseline, so a 12-point gain. However, against a control, the difference was not significant, so the gain could not be attributed to the better aim of the uncertainty-driven camera. My current hypothesis is that this is due to the large camera FOV, the lack of occlusion in the scene, or other factors. I am currently running the experiment again in different conditions with a narrower camera lens as well as random objects to promote occlusion. Will be sharing more results soon!

Autonomous object-tracking drone

UBC MECH410G, Sept–Dec 2025, continued independently until Sept 2026. Flight Control Lead. Bench-tested, not yet flown.

Watch the demo on YouTube

The vision pipeline and ground station running live on the bench.

AOT_DRONE on GitHub

On a three-person mechatronics software course team I wrote the C++ flight control while my teammates wrote the vision (Python) and a desktop ground station (C#). One of them led the hardware for the drone frame, and I soldered the motors to the ESCs and the UART connections for our tests. He and I carried on after the course, and I rebuilt the entire software stack on my own.

The current software stack includes a C++17 flight-control system written for the drone's Raspberry Pi 4. It runs a 50 Hz loop with a four-mode state machine (Manual, Takeoff, Tracking, LandSafely) and fail-closed safety gates, commanding the Betaflight firmware over MSP. For vision I wrote a Python pipeline using YOLOv8n detection and OpenCV KCF/CSRT tracking, which feeds the loop through a FastAPI gateway. The ground station UI is React and TypeScript, showing me live video and telemetry. All the inter-process messaging runs as JSON over UDP or TCP.

If the drone loses its command link for 0.5 s, the stack drops to the LandSafely state. The whole stack is tested in CI with 9 C++ test binaries, about 190 pytest tests and the web app's vitest suite. I also ran a hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) bench test against the real board, which caught a channel-ordering bug the unit tests had missed.

3D-printed radioactive imaging phantoms

UBC capstone for Qurit at the BC Cancer Research Centre, Sept 2024–Apr 2025. Technical Manager. Nominated for top Mechanical Engineering capstone.

We needed to print radioactive phantoms for medical imaging calibration, based on more anatomically realistic organs. The prints had to be water-insoluble, and heat curing was not allowed for radiation safety. We converted the printer (a Rapidia Conflux 1) to print UV-curable resin, with a custom Y-nozzle that allows gradient 3D printing, so the concentration of radioactive tracer can be varied to match the organ model.

I was the Technical Manager: I owned the technical documentation and process, and worked with two teammates to translate our stakeholders' requests into measurable requirements. I also owned the software. I adapted the Python pipeline that turns a PET scan into printable bodies for each gradient, and adapted the G-code post-processor to our new UV panel, cutting the curing passes on an eight-gradient print from 90 per layer to 12. I also rewrote the printer's start and end G-code, which took a 30-minute bake off every print.

On the hardware side I designed and modelled the Y-nozzle in SolidWorks, iterating through several designs and prototyping them as printed PLA parts. The two input streams had to mix inside the nozzle to come out as one blend, and a simple Y-body proved to do the trick. I waterjet-cut the blanks and machined them into our Y-nozzle on the milling machine.

We found out early that our printer was the wrong type of printer for resin, so most of the project was working through problems that kept it from extruding. Cold resin, calibration, and a rotor that kept seizing until we traced it to sealing rings swelling on contact with the resin were the main ones. After I replaced the seals, the machine printed a gradient for the first time on March 30, and the cubes came out before dawn the next morning, after I stayed in the lab overnight.

During our tests we used food dye to stand in for the radioisotope and to see our mixture. Our system printed eight concentration-level cubes in 3.5 hours, which the team extrapolated to roughly 14 to 15 hours for a full organ print. We came in 23% under our client's budget. I also co-wrote the requirement set the design was scored against, and wrote the extrusion and software reports, the operating procedure we handed over with the system, and every set of meeting minutes across the seven-month project. Qurit has since printed with live radioisotope on it, and called me for a while after the handover with troubleshooting questions.

Coddle

Co-founder and project lead, Aug 2025 to now. Shipped to a TestFlight closed beta in summer 2026.

Coddle is an AI cooking companion app my co-founder and I shipped to a closed beta. It keeps track of your kitchen inventory after an initial input from a photo of your fridge or a receipt, or by typing. It matches your inventory against existing recipes to tell you what you can make, or generates new recipes just for you. Cooking mode walks you through the steps with tips and independent timers, so you can work on several steps at once, and then updates your inventory with what you used. Photographing your inventory was our best feature: it put in 202 of the 211 ingredients our testers added, and of the 202 items the model read off their shelves, they corrected two.

I built the backend for the app: the database design and 19 of its 23 migrations, the API, authentication, rate limiting, search and the deployment. On the app I own most of the UI/UX, including the inventory, browse, cook and cookbook tabs, the recipe page, cooking mode and the food icons.

I also built our PostHog analytics, which we use to monitor usage and features. In our beta I found a validation-cap bug that was blocking a tester through our dashboard, traced it, and wrote the fix with six regression tests. The beta is still up for friends and family, and we hope to develop Coddle further for a larger audience.

Coddle is at coddle.app.
